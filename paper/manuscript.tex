\begin{abstract}
Machine-learning surrogates could let designers of metal--organic framework (MOF) water
harvesters screen many candidate adsorbents without solving the transport equations for
each, but only if they work on materials they were not trained on. A surrogate built on a
reduced basis can fail there for two reasons: the basis cannot represent the true field,
or the map from material parameters to basis coefficients predicts the wrong
coefficients. We measure which one limits accuracy for a one-dimensional, non-isothermal
packed-bed model of water uptake, verified against \nLzeroNchecks{} closed-form solutions
and compared, unfitted, with a published MOF-303 breakthrough curve. Seven explanations
of the error were registered in advance and tested on \nNclustFolds\ held-out adsorbents
with a cluster bootstrap that pays for choosing the best configuration on the test set.
The map error is \(\nMtRatioEight\times\) the basis error at eight modes and
\(\nMtRatioHigh\times\) at 128; refining the basis \(\nLfiveVtwoFloorDrop\times\) leaves it
unchanged and improves a jointly trained DeepONet by \nLfiveVtwoGainPct\,\%, all of it by 16
modes. More training materials still help (error \(\propto N_{\mathrm{mat}}^{-\nLCbeta}\)).
Yet a coarse finite-volume solve is \(\nCvsRatio\times\) more accurate than the best
surrogate and cheaper below about \nCvsBeProd\ queries, or \nCvsBeCoarse\ if the training
data are generated coarsely too. Both surrogates still rank held-out materials well
enough for a screen (Spearman \nScrDonSp\ for DeepONet), though the coarse solve ranks
better; on six published frameworks they fail wherever the adsorption step is steeper
than in any training material. For this column, a coarse solve is the screening tool;
surrogate effort belongs in the coefficient map and in training designs that cover real
frameworks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Water-harvesting metal--organic frameworks take up water in a steep, cooperative step at
a characteristic relative humidity, and that step is what lets a device such as MOF-303
or MOF-801 collect water from desert air
\cite{kim2017water,kim2018adsorption,hanikel2021evolution}. Whether a framework makes a
good harvester depends on how a packed bed of it behaves over time, which is set by the
isotherm, the heat released and the kinetics together
\cite{lapotin2019adsorption}. Answering that question for many candidates means solving
transport equations many times, and machine-learning surrogates promise to replace those
solves for materials that have never been simulated
\cite{pai2020generalized,leperi2019development,burns2020prediction,han2025machine,han2025optimizing}.
Machine learning already ranks frameworks by their water uptake from structure
\cite{alkhatib2026machine,arjmandi2026next}, and the closest process-level work trains a
neural network on simulations of a CO\(_2\) capture cycle to map step-isotherm parameters to
energy use and productivity \cite{nasiri2026process,nasiri2026surrogate}; neither tests
transfer to held-out materials or compares against a cheaper solve. Structure-to-isotherm
models are making the isotherm itself predictable from a framework's structure
\cite{goeminne2025ab}; what a surrogate then has to do is map those isotherm and bed
parameters to the dynamic behaviour of the bed.

Most such surrogates write the field as a weighted sum of stored patterns, a reduced
basis, and learn the weights from the material's parameters: proper orthogonal
decomposition (POD) with a regressor \cite{hesthaven2018non,bhattacharya2021model}, and
neural operators with a linear reconstruction such as DeepONet \cite{lu2021learning}. On a
material the model has not seen, such a surrogate can fail in exactly two ways: the
basis cannot represent the true field, or the predicted weights are wrong. Theory bounds
the first for transport-dominated problems \cite{lanthaler2023nonlinear} and separates
the two in principle \cite{bhattacharya2021model,wang2025rbdeeponet}, and Heinlein and
Taraz found the second dominating for DeepONets on model equations
\cite{heinlein2026error}. Which one limits accuracy decides where method development
should go, but it has not been measured for adsorption beds, or for transfer across
materials.

We measure it. The system is a one-dimensional, non-isothermal model of water uptake
in a packed bed, with an S-shaped isotherm that produces the two-wave breakthrough of
real harvesting frameworks. Seven candidate explanations of the error were registered
before the runs, each with the evidence that would eliminate it, and tested on a
240-material design in which every material is held out once. The statistics follow the
standards Digital Discovery's benchmarking work has set for materials models
\cite{witman2025matfold,abaei2026benchmark,burark2024codbench}: materials, not samples,
are the resampling unit, the significance level is calibrated by simulation, and choosing
the best configuration on the test materials is paid for in the interval. Because the
strongest objection to learned surrogates is that a simple numerical method is often
better \cite{mcgreivy2024weak,wyder2025common,dehoop2022cost,huang2026cost}, a coarse solve of the same
equations is one of the baselines.

Three questions organise the paper. What limits accuracy on held-out materials, the
basis or the map? When is a surrogate worth building at all, against a coarse solve?
And do the surrogates rank materials correctly, including six published water-harvesting
frameworks, which is what a screen needs? We report negative results with the same
weight as positive ones, and we state where our model of the adsorbent is too simple.

\section{Model, reference solver and an experimental check}
\label{sec:model}

\paragraph{Governing equations} The bed is a one-dimensional column with axial
dispersion, linear-driving-force (LDF) kinetics and an energy balance with wall loss.
With \(c(z,t)\) the water concentration in the gas, \(q(z,t)\) the loading, \(T(z,t)\)
the temperature and \(v\) the superficial velocity,
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial c}{\partial t} &= D_L \frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial z^2}
 - \frac{v}{\varepsilon_t}\frac{\partial c}{\partial z}
 - \frac{1-\varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t}\,\rho_p \frac{\partial q}{\partial t},
\label{eq:mass}\\
\frac{\partial q}{\partial t} &= k_{\mathrm{LDF}}\bigl(q^{*}(c,T) - q\bigr),
\label{eq:ldf}\\
C_{\mathrm{b}}\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} &= k_z \frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial z^2}
 - v\rho_g C_{pg}\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}
 + (1-\varepsilon_t)\rho_p(-\Delta H)\frac{\partial q}{\partial t}
 - \frac{4h_w}{D_{\mathrm{in}}}\,(T - T_w),
\label{eq:energy}
\end{align}
with Danckwerts flux conditions at the inlet, zero gradients at the outlet and a clean
bed at the feed temperature. The kinetic coefficient is derived from the pellet by
Glueckauf's relation for macropore diffusion \cite{glueckauf1947theory,glueckauf1955theory},
so kinetics and pellet size stay consistent; every material in the dataset is therefore
close to equilibrium (per-material Damk\"ohler number \(\geq \nDaMatMin\)), and a single
LDF constant is an approximation for strongly nonlinear isotherms \cite{sircar2000why}.
The bed heat capacity omits the adsorbed phase. Supplementary S2 gives the fixed
properties, the discretisation and the full parameter table.

\paragraph{The isotherm, and what its parameters mean} Water uptake is described by a
Langmuir term that carries Henry's law plus a cooperative Sips term that produces the
step,
\begin{equation}
q^{*}(c,T) = q_{\max}\left[
f_H\,\frac{b_H c}{1 + b_H c}
+ (1-f_H)\,\frac{(b_C c)^{n}}{1 + (b_C c)^{n}} \right],
\label{eq:isotherm}
\end{equation}
with both affinities sharing the heat of adsorption \(\Delta H\), so Clausius--Clapeyron
holds at every loading, and \(b_C\) set so that the step sits at the material's step
humidity. Single-site Langmuir cannot produce a step, and a bare Sips form violates
Henry's law; the two-term construction follows Do and Do \cite{dodo2000model}. One choice
in this parameterisation matters for what the parameters mean, and an earlier version of
this work described it wrongly. The affinity of the first term is fixed at
\(b_H = b_C/\nDcBH\), so those sites are \emph{weaker} than the cooperative ones: at the
step they are only \nIbsFill\,\% filled, and they supply a median \nIbsShareMed\,\% of the
uptake below the step, most of which comes from the tail of the Sips term. The parameter
\(f_H\) is therefore the capacity fraction of weak Langmuir sites, not the fraction a
framework takes up below its step, and in real MOF-303 the low-humidity sites bind more
strongly than the pore-filling ones \cite{hanikel2021evolution}. This does not affect any
surrogate-versus-solver comparison, which only needs the solver to define the target, but
it limits how literally the materials can be read as real frameworks, and it explains the
anchor result below.

\paragraph{Verification} The solver reproduces \nLzeroNchecks{} closed-form solutions, each
isolating one term (an inert tracer against van Genuchten and Alves's third-type solution
\cite{vangenuchten1982analytical}, a retarded front, a thermal wave and the
Anzelius--Schumann solution for LDF kinetics \cite{anzelius1926uber,schumann1929heat}),
closes the global mass balance to \(\nMassClosure\,\%\), and converges to
\(\nGridConv\,\%\) at \(N_z = \nGridNz\) cells. Every surrogate error below is measured
against this solver.

\begin{figure}[!tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/Fig7_anchor.pdf}
\caption{The reference solver against a published MOF-303 bed \cite{lassitter2024mass},
with no parameter fitted to the curve. Blue: the pre-registered run with the cited
MOF-303 isotherm. Grey: pre-declared variants of the quantities the source does not give
(kinetic constant, pellet size, thermal coupling). Dashed: the authors' own model with

\label{fig:anchor}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{An experimental check, and what it shows} Lassitter and co-workers report a
MOF-303 breakthrough whose bed and conditions are fully specified
\cite{lassitter2024mass}. With nothing tuned, the solver gives 50\,\% breakthrough at
\nAnchorTfiftyModel\ minutes against \nAnchorTfiftyExp\ measured and an intermediate
plateau at \nAnchorPlateauModel\ of the feed against \nAnchorPlateauExp, with an nRMSE of
\nAnchorNrmse\ against the effluent (the authors' own model with fitted kinetics reaches
\nAnchorNrmseComsol; Fig.~\ref{fig:anchor}). The half-breakthrough time is largely a
capacity check. The dynamics are not reproduced: the model's outlet leaks at
\nAnchorTfiveModel\ minutes against \nAnchorTfiveExp\ measured. In our MOF-303
parameterisation the low-humidity sites hold only \nIbsMofFeed\,\% of capacity at the
feed humidity, so almost nothing retains the first wave; a stronger primary site is the
fix, and we have not made it here. The bed is also only about \nAnchorPelletsDeep{} pellets
deep, where the dispersion correlation overstates spreading (Supplementary S2, Section S9).
We treat this comparison as an anchor that bounds how physically the reference can be
read, not as a validation.

\section{Data and evaluation design}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Dataset} The dataset contains \nDataNsamples\ accepted simulations: \nDataNmaterials\
materials crossed with \nDataNconditions\ operating conditions, each set drawn by a
scrambled Sobol sequence (Table~S1). A material is eight numbers: capacity, heat of
adsorption, step humidity, step steepness \(n\) and \(f_H\) for the isotherm, and pellet
size, pellet density and bed porosity, which describe the formulation rather than the
framework. The conditions are feed humidity, velocity and temperature. Fields are
stored as \(c/c_{\mathrm{in}}\), \(q/q_{\max}\) and \(T/T_{\mathrm{in}}\) and scored on a
\(128 \times 128\) grid in \(z/L\) and \(t/t_{\mathrm{final}}\).

\paragraph{Held-out materials} The 240 materials are split into five folds; each surrogate is
fitted, basis and scalers included, on four folds and scored on the fifth, so every
material is held out once and every sample is scored out of fold. The held-out materials
are new points drawn from the same parameter ranges as the training materials, so this is
transfer within a design space, not to new chemistries; Section~\ref{sec:frameworks}
tests six real frameworks, of which only MOF-303 and MIL-160(Al) lie wholly inside it.

\paragraph{Metric and test} The primary metric is the per-sample normalised root-mean-square
error of the concentration field,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{nRMSE}_c(s) = \frac{\bigl[\tfrac{1}{N_zN_t}\sum_{i,j}(\hat c_{ij}-c_{ij})^2\bigr]^{1/2}}
{\max_{i,j} c_{ij} - \min_{i,j} c_{ij}} ,
\label{eq:nrmse}
\end{equation}
averaged with equal weight per sample. Two arms are compared on identical samples by a
paired percentile bootstrap that resamples whole materials, because the conditions of one
material are not independent. In simulated nulls with this design's cluster sizes the
bootstrap rejects \nFprFoldsNominal\,\% of the time at a nominal 5\,\% level, so we test at
\(\alpha = \nAlphaFolds\), whose simulated size is \nFprFoldsCal\,\%. Where a configuration
is chosen as the best of a grid on the held-out materials, its interval is recomputed to
hold for every configuration at once (a studentised max-statistic cluster bootstrap),
and every ``no difference'' is reported with its minimum detectable effect or with the
range of effects its interval admits. The intervals condition on the fitted models: they
resample materials, not training runs, so training variability across folds and seeds is
reported separately where it matters \cite{nadeau2003inference}.

\paragraph{Pre-registration} Each rung's hypothesis, arms, analysis and verdict wording were
written and committed to the project's repository before its runs; amendments made
before a verdict are dated and listed in Supplementary S1. The ordering is attested by
the repository's commit history, not by an external registry, and the history was
rewritten once, before the repository was made public; Table~S2 maps each
pre-registration to its commit and states whether its order survived that rewrite. The
two additions made for this revision (Sections~\ref{sec:screening} and
\ref{sec:frameworks}) were registered and pushed to the public repository before they ran.
Three early comparisons (rungs L3 and part of L5) used an earlier dataset with only
\nNclustLegacy\ held-out materials, where the minimum detectable effect is \nLegacyMDE\,\%;
they are labelled exploratory wherever they appear.

\paragraph{Use of generative AI} A generative AI assistant (Claude Opus 5 and Claude Opus
5.5, Anthropic, used through Claude Code) was used as a tool to help draft the manuscript
and the code, with instructions such as ``implement this pre-registered test as
specified'', ``check this paragraph against the results files'' and ``shorten this section
without changing any number''. The first author designed the study, made every decision,
and read, checked and approved all text and code.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The pre-registered explanations}
\label{sec:ladder}

\begin{figure}[!tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/Fig1_ladder.pdf}
\caption{The seven pre-registered explanations and their outcomes. Each row is one
intervention as a percentage of its own control, with its cluster-bootstrap interval;
positive means lower held-out error. Hollow points are exploratory (12 held-out
materials). MLP, multilayer perceptron; KAN, Kolmogorov--Arnold network; FNO, Fourier
neural operator.}
\label{fig:ladder}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} summarises the seven explanations; each verdict below is in the
wording fixed before its run.

\paragraph{L1, more training materials: not eliminated} The best fixed-basis surrogate, a
multilayer perceptron on 64 POD coefficients per channel, reaches \nLoneMLP\ and beats
gradient boosting in all five folds. On a learning curve over training materials, held-out
error falls by \(\nLCratio\times\) from 12 to 192 materials and still improves
significantly at the last step, as \(N_{\mathrm{mat}}^{-\nLCbeta}\) (exponent interval
[\nLCbetaLo, \nLCbetaHi]). Refitting the basis on each subset changes nothing
(\nLClargestFixed\ against \nLClargest\ at 192 materials): none of the gain is in the
basis. A Gaussian process on the same coefficients is \nBgpVerdict\ (\nBgpGp\ against
\nBgpMlp), so the regressor class is not the lever either.

\paragraph{L2, more capacity: not eliminated} Of \nLtwoNconfigs\ configurations swept in both
directions, \nLtwoNUshaped\ sweeps have interior optima, but the random forest is still
improving at its fully grown limit, so the pre-registered rule keeps capacity open. The
best configuration, \nLtwoBestName, is \(\nLtwoVsLonePct\,\%\) better than the L1 setting,
\(\nLtwoSelLo\)--\(\nLtwoSelHi\,\%\) once its selection is paid for.

\paragraph{L3, Kolmogorov--Arnold maps: eliminated (exploratory)} At matched parameter counts,
the perceptron beats both Kolmogorov--Arnold \cite{liu2025kan} families at every budget,
\nLthreeSigSim\ of \nLthreePairs\ comparisons surviving the selection adjustment and none
reversed, on the 12-material design. The literature is split on this comparison
\cite{shukla2024comprehensive,wang2025kinn,rigas2026training}; our statement is narrow.

\paragraph{L4, the governing equations as a training loss: helps at small weights} A fixed
residual weight of \(10^{\nLfourbBestWeightExp}\) improves transfer to \nLfourbMatNmat\
held-out materials by \nLfourbMatGainPct\,\% over an identical data-only network (interval
over every weighting tried: [\nLfourbMatSelLo, \nLfourbMatSelHi]); every larger weight and
every adaptive weighting scheme is worse, and minimising the residual at inference makes
predictions about \nLfourbRefTwinFactor\ times worse. This agrees with physics-informed
surrogates of adsorption processes reported elsewhere \cite{galanti2025towards} and rests
on a single split.

\paragraph{L5, a better operator: a small gain from the basis, complete by 16 modes}
DeepONet improves by \nLfiveVtwoGainPct\,\% from \(p = 8\) to \(p = 128\) on the 240-material
design, interval [\nLfiveVtwoSelLo, \nLfiveVtwoSelHi] after paying for the learning-rate
choice, and the whole gain is reached by \(p = 16\). A Fourier neural operator
\cite{li2021fourier} is more accurate by point estimate on the 12-material design, but not
significantly once its selection is paid for (exploratory).

\paragraph{L6, separating kinetics from equilibrium: a real benefit, not the predicted one}
Separate equilibrium, kinetic and transport heads beat a joint network by \nLsixDiff\
(interval [\nLsixLo, \nLsixHi]), in all folds and seeds, but the benefit shows no
dependence on Damk\"ohler number, which is what the hypothesis predicted; the dataset never
reaches kinetic control, so that null is scoped to near-equilibrium beds.

\paragraph{L7, closed forms: eliminated} Unfitted, the best classical approximation
(Klinkenberg's \cite{klinkenberg1948numerical,klinkenberg1954heat}) is
\(\nLsevenRatio\times\) worse than learning on the outlet curve; with two physically
interpretable corrections fitted on the training materials it is still
\(\nBfitRatio\times\) worse.

\subsection{Where the error comes from: the coefficient map}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\begin{figure}[!tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/Fig_basismap.pdf}
\caption{Basis error against map error on the 240-material design. \textbf{A}~The two terms
of equation~\eqref{eq:split}, measured separately, against basis size: the basis floor
falls by orders of magnitude while the map term does not move. \textbf{B}~DeepONet, which
trains basis and coefficients together, against basis size. \textbf{C}~The held-out
\(R^2\) of the map for each mode, by number of training materials.}
\label{fig:basismap}
\end{figure}

For a surrogate whose prediction \(\hat u\) lies in the span of an orthonormal basis
\(\{\phi_k\}_{k\le p}\), each sample's error splits exactly into two orthogonal parts,
\begin{equation}
\lVert u - \hat u \rVert^2 \;=\; \underbrace{\lVert u - \Pi_p u \rVert^2}_{\text{basis floor}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{k \le p} \bigl(c_k - \hat c_k\bigr)^2}_{\text{map}},
\qquad c_k = \langle u, \phi_k \rangle ,
\label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
the projection identity that underlies error analyses of POD-based networks
\cite{bhattacharya2021model,wang2025rbdeeponet}. We measured both terms separately for POD
with per-mode gradient boosting and with ridge regression, each regressor fixed in
advance, so neither is chosen on the held-out materials.

The map term dominates at every basis size (Fig.~\ref{fig:basismap}A). At eight modes it
is already \(\nMtRatioEight\times\) the basis floor; at 128 modes the floor has fallen
\(\nLfiveVtwoFloorDrop\times\) and the map term is \(\nMtRatioHigh\times\) larger. The map
term itself barely moves with basis size (\nMtMapEight\ at \(p = 8\), \nMtMapHigh\ at
\(p = 128\)), which for regressors fitted mode by mode is expected: the leading
coefficients are predicted identically at every \(p\), and adding modes adds only their
own small errors. The informative comparison is therefore the ratio of the two terms, and
DeepONet, whose basis is trained together with its coefficients: it gains
\nLfiveVtwoGainPct\,\% from \(p = 8\) to \(16\) and nothing after
(Fig.~\ref{fig:basismap}B). At eight modes DeepONet is only
\(\nLfiveVtwoXfloorEight\times\) above its own floor, so the basis does bind there; by
128 modes it is \(\nLfiveVtwoXfloorHigh\times\) above it. Beyond the first few modes,
accuracy on held-out materials is limited by the map.

The map is limited by data, not by the regressor class: on held-out materials only the
leading modes are predicted well, and the number of modes whose map clears
\(R^2 > 0.2\) grows from \nLCmodesSmall\ to \nLCmodesLarge\ as training materials go from 12
to 192, still rising (Fig.~\ref{fig:basismap}C). The POD residual decays as
\(k^{\nNwidthExpErr}\), so the basis is far from exhausted; the Kolmogorov \(n\)-width
bound our protocol predicted would bind is real but inactive.

\subsection{When a surrogate is worth building}
\label{sec:cost}

The relevant competitor of a surrogate is not the production solve but a cheaper solve of
the same equations. A coarse finite-volume solve with \(N_z = \nCoarseNz\) cells costs
\nCoarseSolveEq\ production solves and reaches \nCvsCoarse\,\% error on the held-out
materials, against \nCvsDon\,\% for the best surrogate, DeepONet at \(p = 16\): the coarse
solve is \(\nCvsRatio\times\) more accurate (paired interval of the difference
[\nCvsLo, \nCvsHi], 240 materials), and \(\nCvsMlpRatio\times\) more accurate than the L1
perceptron. Failed coarse solves are counted at the cost of one production solve, never
dropped.

Accuracy aside, a surrogate repays its training set only after enough queries. Counting
each training simulation as one production solve, the best surrogate becomes cheaper than
coarse solving after about \nCvsBeProd\ queries; generating the training data on the
coarse grid as well, which is legitimate because the coarse solve is the more accurate of
the two, lowers that to about \nCvsBeCoarse. Both are lower bounds, because the fit and the
query are not counted. A screen of a few thousand candidates on this column is therefore
done better and more cheaply by a coarse solve. The balance can turn for two- or
three-dimensional, multi-cycle device models, where a solve costs far more; we have not
measured that case. This is the adsorption-bed instance of a pattern found for neural
operators in general \cite{dehoop2022cost,huang2026cost}, and it agrees with the use of
reduced-physics models for rapid adsorbent screening \cite{liske2026extended}.

\subsection{Do surrogates rank materials correctly?}
\label{sec:screening}

\begin{figure}[!tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/Fig_screening.pdf}
\caption{Screening and named frameworks. \textbf{A}~Spearman correlation between predicted
and true half-breakthrough time, ranking the held-out materials within each operating
condition (small points, one per condition), with the mean and its cluster-bootstrap
interval (squares); the dotted line is the pre-registered threshold. \textbf{B}~Top-10\,\%
recall, same layout. \textbf{C}~Field error on the six named frameworks (blue, DeepONet;
orange, POD with gradient boosting; black, coarse solve); hollow markers lie outside the sampled design space, and dashed lines mark the 95th
percentile of each surrogate's per-material error on the held-out materials.}
\label{fig:screening}
\end{figure}

A screen needs the order of the candidates more than their fields. Within each of the
17 operating conditions we ranked the held-out materials by their half-breakthrough time
\(t_{50}\), the time at which the outlet first reaches half the feed concentration, and
scored each arm by the Spearman correlation with the true order and by top-10\,\% recall,
the share of the true top tenth that the arm also places in its top tenth. Both metrics,
the arms and the verdict thresholds were registered before the runs: a surrogate is
\emph{screening-adequate} if the lower ends of its intervals reach 0.90 for Spearman and
0.80 for recall.

Both surrogates are screening-adequate (Fig.~\ref{fig:screening}A,B). DeepONet reaches a
Spearman correlation of \nScrDonSp\ (interval [\nScrDonSpLo, \nScrDonSpHi]) and a recall of
\nScrDonRc\,\% ([\nScrDonRcLo, \nScrDonRcHi]); POD with gradient boosting reaches \nScrHgbSp\
([\nScrHgbSpLo, \nScrHgbSpHi]) and \nScrHgbRc\,\% ([\nScrHgbRcLo, \nScrHgbRcHi]). The field
error that dominates the previous sections therefore does not stop a surrogate from
ordering candidates; ranking by one scalar is an easier task than predicting the field.
The coarse solve is better at both. It ranks almost perfectly (Spearman \nScrCoSp, recall
\nScrCoRc\,\%, lower end \nScrCoRcLo\,\%), and both surrogates rank significantly worse
than it: per hundred true top candidates, DeepONet misses between \nScrDonDrcLo\ and
\nScrDonDrcHi\ more and POD with boosting between \nScrHgbDrcLo\ and \nScrHgbDrcHi\ more
(paired intervals). Over all \nScrNsamples\ samples the field errors are \nScrDonNr\,\% for
DeepONet, \nScrHgbNr\,\% for POD with boosting and \nScrCoNr\,\% for the coarse solve, at a
mean cost of \nScrCoCost\ production solves per sample with failed coarse solves charged
in full.

\subsection{Six published frameworks}
\label{sec:frameworks}

The held-out materials above are random points of the sampled box. To test real
adsorbents, six water-harvesting frameworks were parameterised from their primary
literature by a rule fixed before the runs: capacity, heat of adsorption and step humidity
as the means of the stated values, and the step steepness from the stated rise interval,
\(n = \ln 81/\ln(p_{90}/p_{10})\); the formulation parameters and \(f_H\), which are not
framework properties in this model, take the dataset medians. Each framework was solved
at the 17 dataset conditions (the dataset's own filter rejected \nFwNcellsFailed\ cells,
in which the feed loads almost none of the capacity), and both surrogates were refit
once on all 240 materials. A framework is \emph{inside the held-out error distribution}
when its mean error is at most the 95th percentile of the same arm's per-material errors
in the five-fold test (\nFwDonPninetyfive\,\% for DeepONet, \nFwHgbPninetyfive\,\% for POD
with boosting).

include their steep steps.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The error budget} Three error levels sit side by side in this work: a surrogate
against the solver (about \nCvsDon\,\% at best), a coarse solve against the solver
(\nCvsCoarse\,\%) and the solver against a real MOF-303 bed (\nAnchorNrmse). For screening
real frameworks the largest term is the model of the adsorbent, not the learned surrogate.
Better isotherm and kinetic parameterisations, including stronger low-humidity sites and
micropore-controlled kinetics \cite{hastings2024high,sircar2000why}, would do more for a
real screen than a better surrogate of the present model.

\paragraph{What to improve} Where surrogates are worth building, the measurements point
to the coefficient map and to the diversity of training materials, not to richer bases,
Kolmogorov--Arnold layers or heavy physics losses. Joint training of basis and
coefficients, as in DeepONet, is worth a constant factor over per-mode regression on the
same coefficients, and how the parameters enter a model governs how far it extrapolates
\cite{vantegelen2026late}; structure-aware inputs to the map, for example isotherms predicted
from framework structure \cite{goeminne2025ab}, are the natural next lever. The
named-framework test adds a plainer requirement: the training design must span the steep
steps of real frameworks, since a surrogate that is accurate inside its box is not
accurate on CAU-10-H or aluminium fumarate.

\paragraph{Limitations} The held-out materials are drawn from the same design space, so
the main results are interpolation statements; the named-framework test probes
extrapolation for six materials only. The model describes one adsorption step from a clean
bed, near equilibrium, in one dimension, with no desorption, cycling or
heat-transfer-limited device geometry \cite{lapotin2019adsorption}. The isotherm's
low-humidity sites are weaker than real ones, and its step steepness range is far below
that of real frameworks. Benchmark conclusions for MOF models can change with sample size
\cite{abaei2026benchmark}, which is why the 12-material comparisons are labelled
exploratory. The pre-registrations are attested by the author's own repository history,
not an external registry.

%% ===========================================================================
\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

For surrogates of a MOF water-adsorption bed, tested on held-out materials under a
pre-registered, selection-corrected design, the error is set by the map from material
parameters to basis coefficients: it is many times the basis error at every basis size, and
a \(\nLfiveVtwoFloorDrop\times\) better basis does not reduce it. More training materials reduce it and are
still paying at the largest size we could afford. For this one-dimensional column a coarse
numerical solve is both more accurate and, for screens below a few thousand candidates,
cheaper than the best surrogate. Surrogates nevertheless rank candidates well enough for a
screen, and the named frameworks inside the design space, MOF-303 and MIL-160(Al), are
predicted as accurately as the held-out materials; frameworks whose steps are steeper than
any in the training design are not. The measurement protocol carries to
other parametric surrogates, and the code, data and pre-registrations are public.
