\begin{abstract}
Machine-learning surrogates could let designers of water-harvesting metal--organic
frameworks (MOFs) screen candidates without solving the transport equations for each, but
only if they work on materials they were not trained on. A surrogate built on a reduced
basis fails there either because the basis cannot represent the true field or because the
map from material parameters to basis coefficients is wrong. We measure which, for a
verified packed-bed model of water uptake, on two pre-registered designs: smooth isotherms,
and MOF-like isotherms with strong low-humidity sites and steep steps. On held-out
materials the coefficient map carries at least \nShMin\,\% of the squared error, for proper
orthogonal decomposition with gradient boosting and for DeepONet measured against its own
trained basis; a richer basis changes nothing, while more training materials help. A coarse
finite-volume solve is \nScrCoRatio\ to \nScrVthreeCoRatio\ times more accurate than the best
surrogate, which repays its training only after about \nCostBeFit\ queries. Surrogates rank
smooth-isotherm materials well enough for a screen but not MOF-like ones, where only a solve
ranks reliably. Trained without steep steps they fail on CAU-10-H and aluminium fumarate;
trained with them they predict six published frameworks within their held-out error.
Surrogate effort should go into the coefficient map and into training designs that cover
real frameworks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Water-harvesting metal--organic frameworks take up water in a steep, cooperative step at
a characteristic relative humidity, and that step is what lets a device such as MOF-303
or MOF-801 collect water from desert air
\cite{kim2017water,kim2018adsorption,hanikel2021evolution}. Whether a framework makes a
good harvester depends on how a packed bed of it behaves over time, which is set by the
isotherm, the heat released and the kinetics together
\cite{lapotin2019adsorption}. Answering that question for many candidates means solving
transport equations many times, and machine-learning surrogates promise to replace those
solves for materials that have never been simulated
\cite{leperi2019development,burns2020prediction,han2025machine,han2025optimizing}. An
adsorbent-agnostic network already takes isotherm parameters as input and predicts the
performance indicators of cyclic processes for unseen adsorbents, validated on two
zeolites \cite{pai2020generalized,pai2022experimental}, and DeepONets have been trained on
cyclic adsorption with travelling fronts \cite{ceccanti2026deep}. For water-harvesting
frameworks, machine learning ranks materials by their uptake from structure
\cite{alkhatib2026machine,arjmandi2026next,abdelwadood2026ai}, isotherm shape has been
linked to regeneration in a cycling model \cite{bezrukov2023metal}, and the closest
process-level surrogate maps step-isotherm parameters of a CO\(_2\) capture cycle to energy
use and productivity \cite{nasiri2026process,nasiri2026surrogate}. None of these predicts
the breakthrough field of held-out materials or compares against a cheaper solve.
Structure-to-isotherm models are making the isotherm itself predictable
\cite{goeminne2025ab}; what a surrogate then has to do is map those isotherm and bed
parameters to the dynamic behaviour of the bed.

Most such surrogates write the field as a weighted sum of stored patterns, a reduced
basis, and learn the weights from the material's parameters: proper orthogonal
decomposition (POD) with a regressor \cite{hesthaven2018non,bhattacharya2021model}, and
neural operators with a linear reconstruction such as DeepONet \cite{lu2021learning}. On a
material the model has not seen, such a surrogate can fail in exactly two ways: the
basis cannot represent the true field, or the predicted weights are wrong. Theory bounds
the first for transport-dominated problems \cite{lanthaler2023nonlinear} and separates the
two \cite{bhattacharya2021model,lanthaler2022error,brivio2024error,wang2025rbdeeponet}, and
for held-out parameter values of single model problems the coefficient error has been
found to dominate \cite{halder2022non,franco2023mesh,heinlein2026error}. How the two divide
when the held-out unit is a material has not been measured, for adsorption beds or for any
breakthrough model whose isotherms have the low-humidity sites and steep steps of real
water-harvesting frameworks.

Here we measure it. The system is a one-dimensional, non-isothermal model of water uptake
in a packed bed, with an S-shaped isotherm that produces the two-wave breakthrough of
real harvesting frameworks. Seven candidate explanations of the error were registered
before the runs, each with the evidence that would eliminate it, and tested on a
240-material design in which every material is held out once. The central measurement is
then repeated on a second design whose isotherms have the strong low-humidity sites and
steep steps of real water MOFs. The statistics follow the
standards Digital Discovery's benchmarking work has set for materials models
\cite{witman2025matfold,abaei2026benchmark,burark2024codbench}: materials, not samples,
are the resampling and splitting unit \cite{kapoor2023leakage}, the significance level is
calibrated by simulation, and choosing the best configuration on the test materials is
paid for in the interval. Because the strongest objection to learned surrogates is that a
simple numerical method is often better
\cite{mcgreivy2024weak,wyder2025common,chatain2025numerical,huang2026cost}, though not always
\cite{koehler2025neural}, a coarse solve of the same equations is one of the baselines.

The paper asks what limits accuracy on held-out materials, the basis or the map; when a
surrogate is worth building at all, compared with a coarse solve; and whether surrogates
rank materials correctly, including six published water-harvesting frameworks, which is
what a screen needs. We also state where the model of the adsorbent is too simple.
Table~\ref{tab:terms} defines the terms used throughout.

The answers matter to anyone building a computational screen for water-harvesting
frameworks. For a one-dimensional column, a coarse solve already ranks thousands of
candidates in about a CPU-hour, more reliably than any surrogate tested here. Where
surrogates are needed, for device models whose solves cost far more, accuracy on new
materials will come from better coefficient maps and from training designs that contain
the strong sites and steep steps of real frameworks, not from richer bases. And the
diagnostic itself, measuring the basis floor and the map error separately on held-out
materials, applies to any surrogate that builds its output from a basis.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Model, reference solver and an experimental check}
\label{sec:model}

\paragraph{Governing equations} The bed is a one-dimensional column with axial
dispersion, linear-driving-force (LDF) kinetics and an energy balance with wall loss.
With \(c(z,t)\) the water concentration in the gas, \(q(z,t)\) the loading, \(T(z,t)\)
the temperature and \(v\) the superficial velocity,
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial c}{\partial t} &= D_L \frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial z^2}
 - \frac{v}{\varepsilon_t}\frac{\partial c}{\partial z}
 - \frac{1-\varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t}\,\rho_p \frac{\partial q}{\partial t},
\label{eq:mass}\\
\frac{\partial q}{\partial t} &= k_{\mathrm{LDF}}\bigl(q^{*}(c,T) - q\bigr),
\label{eq:ldf}\\
C_{\mathrm{b}}\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} &= k_z \frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial z^2}
 - v\rho_g C_{pg}\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}
 + (1-\varepsilon_t)\rho_p(-\Delta H)\frac{\partial q}{\partial t}
 - \frac{4h_w}{D_{\mathrm{in}}}\,(T - T_w),
\label{eq:energy}
\end{align}
with Danckwerts flux conditions at the inlet, zero gradients at the outlet and a clean
bed at the feed temperature. The kinetic coefficient is derived from the pellet by
Glueckauf's relation for macropore diffusion \cite{glueckauf1947theory,glueckauf1955theory},
so kinetics and pellet size stay consistent; every material in the dataset is therefore
close to equilibrium (per-material Damk\"ohler number \(\geq \nDaMatMin\)), and a single
LDF constant is an approximation for strongly nonlinear isotherms \cite{sircar2000why}.
The bed heat capacity omits the adsorbed phase. Supplementary S2 gives the fixed
properties, the discretisation and the full parameter table.

\paragraph{The isotherm, and what its parameters mean} Water uptake is described by a
Langmuir term that carries Henry's law plus a cooperative Sips term that produces the
step,
\begin{equation}
q^{*}(c,T) = q_{\max}\left[
f_H\,\frac{b_H c}{1 + b_H c}
+ (1-f_H)\,\frac{(b_C c)^{n}}{1 + (b_C c)^{n}} \right],
\label{eq:isotherm}
\end{equation}
with both affinities sharing the heat of adsorption \(\Delta H\), so Clausius--Clapeyron
holds at every loading, and \(b_C\) set so that the step sits at the material's step
humidity. Single-site Langmuir cannot produce a step, and a bare Sips form violates
Henry's law; the two-term construction follows Do and Do \cite{dodo2000model}.

The two designs differ in how strongly the first term binds and how steep the step can be.
In the \emph{smooth} design (v2) the affinity of the first term is fixed at
\(b_H = b_C/\nDcBH\), so those sites are \emph{weaker} than the cooperative ones: at the step
they are only \nIbsFill\,\% filled and supply a median \nIbsShareMed\,\% of the uptake below
the step, and \(n\) is sampled up to \nDcNexpHi. These isotherms give smooth breakthrough
curves, but they are not those of real water MOFs, whose low-humidity sites bind more
strongly than the pore-filling ones \cite{hanikel2021evolution,kalmutzki2018metal} and whose
steps are much steeper (Section~\ref{sec:frameworks}). The \emph{MOF-like} design (v3)
samples \(\log_{10}(b_H/b_C)\) from \nVthreeAffLo\ to \nVthreeAffHi, so primary sites up to
\nVthreeAffHiRatio\ times stronger than the step sites, \(n\) up to \nVthreeNmax, and
capacity up to \nVthreeQmaxHi\ mol\,kg\(^{-1}\), so that its box encloses all six named
frameworks (Table~S1). Everything else, including the solver, is shared.

\paragraph{Verification} The solver reproduces \nLzeroNchecks{} closed-form solutions, each
isolating one term (an inert tracer against van Genuchten and Alves's third-type solution
\cite{vangenuchten1982analytical}, a retarded front, a thermal wave and the
Anzelius--Schumann solution for LDF kinetics \cite{anzelius1926uber,schumann1929heat}),
closes the global mass balance to \(\nMassClosure\,\%\), and converges to
\(\nGridConv\,\%\) at \(N_z = \nGridNz\) cells. Every surrogate error below is measured
against this solver.

\paragraph{An experimental check, and what it shows} Lassitter and co-workers report a
MOF-303 breakthrough whose bed and conditions are fully specified
\cite{lassitter2024mass}. With nothing tuned, the solver gives 50\,\% breakthrough at
\nAnchorTfiftyModel\ minutes against \nAnchorTfiftyExp\ measured and an intermediate
plateau at \nAnchorPlateauModel\ of the feed against \nAnchorPlateauExp, with an nRMSE of
\nAnchorNrmse\ against the effluent (the authors' own model with fitted kinetics reaches
\nAnchorNrmseComsol; Fig.~\ref{fig:anchor}). The half-breakthrough time is largely a
capacity check, and the acceptance criteria declared before the run, which this run meets,
test capacity rather than dynamics; they were too lenient. The dynamics are not
reproduced: the model's outlet leaks at \nAnchorTfiveModel\ minutes against
\nAnchorTfiveExp\ measured. The anchor uses the smooth design's weak primary sites, which
hold only \nIbsMofFeed\,\% of capacity at the feed humidity, so almost nothing retains the
first wave; this is the shortcoming the MOF-like design addresses. An exploratory re-run
confirms it: with primary sites ten times stronger than the step sites and the below-step
fraction the MOF-303 source reports (\(f_H = \nAnchorStrongFh\)), still with nothing fitted,
the first leak moves to \nAnchorStrongTfive\ minutes against \nAnchorTfiveExp\ measured and
the error against the effluent falls to \nAnchorStrongNrmse\ (Fig.~\ref{fig:anchor}). The run is also
isothermal, as the authors' own model is, and takes its kinetic constant from their fitted
values, so it tests neither the energy balance nor the pellet-derived kinetics of the
datasets, and the bed is only about \nAnchorPelletsDeep{} pellets deep, where a
one-dimensional continuum with a dispersion correlation is outside its range
(Supplementary S2, Section S9). It bounds how physically the reference solver can be read;
it is not a validation.

\subsection{Data and evaluation design}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Datasets} The smooth design contains \nDataNsamples\ accepted simulations:
\nDataNmaterials\ materials crossed with \nDataNconditions\ operating conditions, each set
drawn by a scrambled Sobol sequence (Table~S1). A material is eight numbers: capacity, heat
of adsorption, step humidity, step steepness \(n\) and \(f_H\) for the isotherm, and pellet
size, pellet density and bed porosity, which describe the formulation rather than the
framework; the MOF-like design adds the affinity ratio as a ninth. The conditions are feed
humidity, velocity and temperature, and the MOF-like design uses the same \nDataNconditions\
conditions. It contains \nVthreeNsamples\ accepted simulations of \nVthreeNmat\ materials;
\nVthreeNrejected\ draws were rejected by the same pre-solve filter as in the smooth design,
most because a strong-site isotherm is saturated by the feed. Fields are
stored as \(c/c_{\mathrm{in}}\), \(q/q_{\max}\) and \(T/T_{\mathrm{in}}\) and scored on a
\(128 \times 128\) grid in \(z/L\) and \(t/t_{\mathrm{final}}\).

\paragraph{Held-out materials} In each design the materials are split into five folds; each
surrogate is fitted, basis and scalers included, on four folds and scored on the fifth, so
every material is held out once and every sample is scored out of fold. The held-out
materials are new points drawn from the same parameter ranges as the training materials,
so this is transfer within a design space, not to new chemistries;
Section~\ref{sec:frameworks} tests six real frameworks, which lie partly outside the smooth
design and wholly inside the MOF-like one.

\paragraph{Metric and test} The primary metric is the per-sample normalised root-mean-square
error of the concentration field,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{nRMSE}_c(s) = \frac{\bigl[\tfrac{1}{N_zN_t}\sum_{i,j}(\hat c_{ij}-c_{ij})^2\bigr]^{1/2}}
{\max_{i,j} c_{ij} - \min_{i,j} c_{ij}} ,
\label{eq:nrmse}
\end{equation}
averaged with equal weight per sample. Two arms are compared on identical samples by a
paired percentile bootstrap that resamples whole materials, because the conditions of one
material are not independent. In simulated nulls with this design's cluster sizes the
bootstrap rejects \nFprFoldsNominal\,\% of the time at a nominal 5\,\% level, so we test at
\(\alpha = \nAlphaFolds\), whose simulated size is \nFprFoldsCal\,\%. Where a configuration
is chosen as the best of a grid on the held-out materials, its interval is recomputed to
hold for every configuration at once (a studentised max-statistic cluster bootstrap),
and every ``no difference'' is reported with its minimum detectable effect or with the
range of effects its interval admits. The intervals condition on the fitted models: they
resample materials, not training runs, so training variability across folds and seeds is
reported separately where it matters \cite{nadeau2003inference}.

\paragraph{Pre-registration} Each rung's hypothesis, arms, analysis and verdict wording were
written and committed to the project's repository before its runs; amendments made
before a verdict are dated and listed in Supplementary S1. The ordering is attested by
the repository's commit history, not by an external registry, and the history was
rewritten once, before the repository was made public; Table~S2 maps each
pre-registration to its commit and states whether its order survived that rewrite. The
screening and named-framework tests (Sections~\ref{sec:screening} and
\ref{sec:frameworks}) and the MOF-like design with DeepONet's own basis floor
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}) were added after the original seven explanations; each was
registered and pushed to the public repository before any of its results were computed. One
amendment, which corrected the solver for DeepONet's own floor and added the controls of
Sections~\ref{sec:mechanism}--\ref{sec:frameworks}, was written after the first results of the
MOF-like design and is marked as such; Supplementary S1 lists every deviation from the
registered text.
Three early comparisons (the Kolmogorov--Arnold comparison L3 and part of the operator
comparison L5, Section~\ref{sec:ladder}) used an earlier dataset with only
\nNclustLegacy\ held-out materials, where the minimum detectable effect is \nLegacyMDE\,\%;
they are labelled exploratory wherever they appear.

\paragraph{Use of generative AI} A generative AI assistant (Claude Opus 5 and Claude Opus
5.5, Anthropic, used through Claude Code) was used as a tool to help draft the manuscript
and the code, with instructions such as ``implement this pre-registered test as
specified'', ``check this paragraph against the results files'' and ``shorten this section
without changing any number''. The first author designed the study, made every decision,
and read, checked and approved all text and code.

\subsection{The pre-registered explanations}
\label{sec:ladder}

\begin{figure}[!tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/Fig1_ladder.pdf}
\caption{The seven pre-registered explanations and their outcomes. Each row is one
intervention as a percentage of its own control, with its cluster-bootstrap interval;
positive means lower held-out error. Hollow points are exploratory (12 held-out
materials). MLP, multilayer perceptron; KAN, Kolmogorov--Arnold network; FNO, Fourier
neural operator.}
\label{fig:ladder}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} summarises the seven explanations; each verdict below is in the
wording fixed before its run.

\paragraph{L1, more training materials: not eliminated} The best fixed-basis surrogate, a
multilayer perceptron on 64 POD coefficients per channel, reaches \nLoneMLP\ and beats
gradient boosting in all five folds. On a learning curve over training materials, held-out
error falls by \(\nLCratio\times\) from 12 to 192 materials and still improves
significantly at the last step, as \(N_{\mathrm{mat}}^{-\nLCbeta}\) (exponent interval
[\nLCbetaLo, \nLCbetaHi]). Refitting the basis on each subset changes nothing
(\nLClargestFixed\ against \nLClargest\ at 192 materials): none of the gain is in the
basis. A Gaussian process on the same coefficients finds \nBgpVerdict\ (\nBgpGp\ against
\nBgpMlp; the smallest difference the test could detect is \nBgpMde\,\%).

\paragraph{L2, more capacity: not eliminated} Of \nLtwoNconfigs\ configurations swept in both
directions, \nLtwoNUshaped\ sweeps have interior optima, but the random forest is still
improving at its fully grown limit, so the pre-registered rule keeps capacity open. The
best configuration, \nLtwoBestName, is \(\nLtwoVsLonePct\,\%\) better than the L1 setting,
\(\nLtwoSelLo\)--\(\nLtwoSelHi\,\%\) once its selection is paid for.

\paragraph{L3, Kolmogorov--Arnold maps: eliminated (exploratory)} At matched parameter counts,
the perceptron beats both Kolmogorov--Arnold \cite{liu2025kan} families at every budget,
\nLthreeSigSim\ of \nLthreePairs\ comparisons surviving the selection adjustment and none
reversed, on the 12-material design. The literature is split on this comparison
\cite{shukla2024comprehensive,wang2025kinn,rigas2026training}; our statement is narrow.

\paragraph{L4, the governing equations as a training loss: helps at small weights} A fixed
residual weight of \(10^{\nLfourbBestWeightExp}\) improves transfer to \nLfourbMatNmat\
held-out materials by \nLfourbMatGainPct\,\% over an identical data-only network (interval
over every weighting tried: [\nLfourbMatSelLo, \nLfourbMatSelHi]); every larger weight and
every adaptive weighting scheme is worse, and minimising the residual at inference makes
predictions about \nLfourbRefTwinFactor\ times worse. Physics-informed surrogates of
adsorption processes gain most when data are scarce \cite{galanti2025towards}; with the
training data used here the gain is small, and the result rests on a single split.

\paragraph{L5, a better operator: a small gain from the basis, complete by 16 modes}
DeepONet improves by \nLfiveVtwoGainPct\,\% from \(p = 8\) to \(p = 128\) on the 240-material
design, interval [\nLfiveVtwoSelLo, \nLfiveVtwoSelHi] after paying for the learning-rate
choice, and the whole gain is reached by \(p = 16\). A Fourier neural operator
\cite{li2021fourier} is more accurate by point estimate on the 12-material design, but not
significantly once its selection is paid for (exploratory).

\paragraph{L6, separating kinetics from equilibrium: a real benefit, not the predicted one}
Separate equilibrium, kinetic and transport heads beat a joint network by \nLsixDiff\
(interval [\nLsixLo, \nLsixHi]), in all folds and seeds, but the benefit shows no
dependence on Damk\"ohler number, which is what the hypothesis predicted; the dataset never
reaches kinetic control, so that null is scoped to near-equilibrium beds.

\paragraph{L7, closed forms: eliminated} Unfitted, the best classical approximation
(Klinkenberg's \cite{klinkenberg1948numerical,klinkenberg1954heat}) is
\(\nLsevenRatio\times\) worse than learning on the outlet curve; with two physically
interpretable corrections fitted on the training materials it is still
\(\nBfitRatio\times\) worse.

\subsection{Where the error comes from: the coefficient map}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\begin{figure}[!tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/Fig_basismap.pdf}
\caption{Basis error against map error, on held-out materials of both designs (blue,
smooth isotherms; red, MOF-like isotherms). \textbf{A}~POD with per-mode gradient
boosting: the two terms of equation~\eqref{eq:split}, measured separately; the basis floor
(dashed) falls by orders of magnitude while the map term (solid) does not move.
\textbf{B}~DeepONet: its total error (solid) and its own floor (dashed), the error left
when its trained trunk is kept and the coefficients are fitted to the true field.
\textbf{C}~The share of squared error carried by the map, with cluster-bootstrap
intervals; the registered verdict ``the map dominates'' needs at least half at every basis
size from 16 on. \textbf{D}~Modes whose map reaches a held-out \(R^2\) above 0.2 and 0.5,
by number of training materials (smooth design).}
\label{fig:basismap}
\end{figure}

For a surrogate whose prediction \(\hat u\) lies in the span of an orthonormal basis
\(\{\phi_k\}_{k\le p}\), each sample's error splits exactly into two orthogonal parts,
\begin{equation}
\lVert u - \hat u \rVert^2 \;=\; \underbrace{\lVert u - \Pi_p u \rVert^2}_{\text{basis floor}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{k \le p} \bigl(c_k - \hat c_k\bigr)^2}_{\text{map}},
\qquad c_k = \langle u, \phi_k \rangle ,
\label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
the projection identity behind error decompositions of POD-based networks and neural
operators \cite{bhattacharya2021model,lanthaler2022error,brivio2024error,wang2025rbdeeponet}. We measured both terms
separately for POD with per-mode gradient boosting and with ridge regression, each
regressor fixed in advance, so neither is chosen on the held-out materials. DeepONet needs
more care: its basis is learned rather than orthonormal and is seen through a sigmoid
output, so equation~\eqref{eq:split} does not apply to it directly (a fixed POD trunk would
make it apply \cite{lu2022comprehensive}). We define its own floor as the error left when its
trained trunk \(T\) is kept and the coefficients are fitted to each held-out field,
\(\min_{\mathbf a}\lVert u - \sigma(T\mathbf a + b)\rVert\); its error above that floor is
the error of its coefficient map. Throughout, the comparison is of shares of squared
error, the quantity equation~\eqref{eq:split} adds, and the verdict words were fixed
before the runs: \emph{the map dominates} if it carries at least half of the squared error,
with the lower end of its interval above one half, at every basis size from 16 on.

The map dominates for both models on both designs; all four registered verdicts agree
(Fig.~\ref{fig:basismap}C). For POD with boosting on the smooth design the map carries
\nShHgbVtwoEight\,\% of the squared error at eight modes and \nShHgbVtwoSixteen\,\% at
sixteen; the floor keeps falling, to \nHgbVtwoFloorHigh\,\% at 128 modes, while the map term does
not move (Fig.~\ref{fig:basismap}A). That flatness is expected for regressors fitted mode
by mode, whose leading coefficients are predicted identically at every \(p\), so the
informative quantity is the share, not the ratio of the two terms, which grows without
limit as the floor falls. On the MOF-like design the steep fronts raise the floor
\(\nFloorRatioVthree\times\) (to \nHgbVthreeFloorEight\,\% at eight modes), yet the map still carries
\nShHgbVthreeSixteen\,\% at sixteen. For DeepONet against its own floor the map carries
\nShDonVtwoSixteen\,\% (smooth) and \nShDonVthreeSixteen\,\% (MOF-like) at \(p = 16\), and
on the MOF-like design its trained trunk represents the held-out fields to
\nDonVthreeFloorSixteen\,\%, against a total error of \nDonVthreeTotalSixteen\,\%; growing
the basis to 128 modes leaves that total at \nDonVthreeTotalHigh\,\%
(Fig.~\ref{fig:basismap}B). The steeper fronts of MOF-like isotherms raise the floor but do
not move the limit to the basis.

More training materials reduce the map error: on held-out materials of the smooth design
only the leading modes are predicted well, and the number of modes whose map clears
\(R^2 > 0.2\) grows from \nLCmodesSmall\ to \nLCmodesLarge\ as training materials go from 12
to 192, still rising (Fig.~\ref{fig:basismap}D). The regressor matters too, by a constant
factor: ridge regression has a \(\nRidgeOverHgb\times\) larger map error than boosting, and
DeepONet's field error is \(\nHgbOverDon\times\) smaller than that of POD with boosting.
The POD residual decays as \(k^{\nNwidthExpErr}\), so the basis is far from exhausted, and
the \(n\)-width limit that bounds linear bases for transport-dominated problems
\cite{lanthaler2023nonlinear} is not the active one here.

\subsection{When a surrogate is worth building}
\label{sec:cost}

The relevant competitor of a surrogate is not the production solve but a cheaper solve of
the same equations. A production solve takes \nCostProdS\,s; a coarse finite-volume solve
with \(N_z = \nCoarseNz\) cells takes \nCostCoarseS\,s and reaches \nScrCoNr\,\% error over
all \nScrNsamples\ samples of the smooth design, against \nScrDonNr\,\% for the best
surrogate, DeepONet at \(p = 16\): the coarse solve is \(\nScrCoRatio\times\) more accurate
(paired interval of the difference [\nScrDonMinusCoLo, \nScrDonMinusCoHi] percentage
points). On the MOF-like design the gap widens to \(\nScrVthreeCoRatio\times\)
(\nScrVthreeCoNr\,\% against \nScrVthreeDonNr\,\%). The \nCoFailVtwo\ coarse solves that
failed are re-solved at production resolution and charged as such; this is a policy, not an
exclusion, and Supplementary S1 (entry B87) gives the comparison with them excluded. The
coarse grid was not tuned: with \(N_z = 100\) cells its error falls to
\nCostCoarseHundredErr\,\%, for \nCostCoarseHundredS\,s per solve.

Accuracy aside, a surrogate repays its training only after enough queries. Its
\nCostLargeSims\ training simulations and its fit (\nCostFitS\,s, the cost of about
\nCostFitSolves\ production solves) take \nCostTrainCpuH\ CPU-hours, and it becomes cheaper
than the coarse solve after about \nCostBeFit\ queries, even with its own query time set to
zero. For this column the stakes are therefore small in absolute terms: either route
screens thousands of candidates in about a CPU-hour, and the coarse solve does it more
accurately. The balance can turn for two- or
three-dimensional, multi-cycle device models, where a solve costs far more; we have not
measured that case. The comparison follows the cost--accuracy framing of operator learning
\cite{dehoop2022cost} and agrees with comparisons of neural and numerical solvers at
matched accuracy \cite{huang2026cost,chatain2025numerical}; it also agrees with screening by
reduced-physics models \cite{liske2026extended,grimm2022machine} and with making the solver
itself faster rather than replacing it \cite{glover2026accelerating}.

\subsection{Do surrogates rank materials correctly?}
\label{sec:screening}

A screen needs the order of the candidates more than their fields. Within each of the
17 operating conditions we ranked the held-out materials by their half-breakthrough time
\(t_{50}\), the time at which the outlet first reaches half the feed concentration, and
scored each arm by the Spearman correlation with the true order and by top-10\,\% recall,
the share of the true top tenth that the arm also places in its top tenth. Both metrics,
the arms and the verdict thresholds were registered before the runs: a surrogate is
\emph{screening-adequate} if the lower ends of its intervals reach 0.90 for Spearman and
0.80 for recall.

On the smooth design both surrogates are screening-adequate (Fig.~\ref{fig:screening}A,B).
DeepONet reaches a Spearman correlation of \nScrDonSp\ (interval [\nScrDonSpLo,
\nScrDonSpHi]) and a recall of \nScrDonRc\,\% ([\nScrDonRcLo, \nScrDonRcHi]); POD with
gradient boosting reaches \nScrHgbSp\ ([\nScrHgbSpLo, \nScrHgbSpHi]) and \nScrHgbRc\,\%
([\nScrHgbRcLo, \nScrHgbRcHi]), clearing the recall threshold only narrowly, so ranking by a single scalar is an easier task
than predicting the whole field. The coarse solve is better at both: it ranks
almost perfectly (Spearman \nScrCoSp, recall \nScrCoRc\,\%), and both surrogates rank
significantly worse than it; per hundred true top candidates, DeepONet misses between
\nScrDonDrcLo\ and \nScrDonDrcHi\ more and POD with boosting between \nScrHgbDrcLo\ and
\nScrHgbDrcHi\ more (paired intervals). Two further training seeds leave DeepONet's mean
Spearman between \nSeedSpMin\ and \nSeedSpMax.

On the MOF-like design neither surrogate is screening-adequate. DeepONet's Spearman
correlation falls to \nScrVthreeDonSp\ ([\nScrVthreeDonSpLo, \nScrVthreeDonSpHi]) and its
recall to \nScrVthreeDonRc\,\% ([\nScrVthreeDonRcLo, \nScrVthreeDonRcHi]); POD with boosting
reaches \nScrVthreeHgbSp\ and \nScrVthreeHgbRc\,\%. The coarse solve still ranks almost
perfectly (Spearman \nScrVthreeCoSp, recall \nScrVthreeCoRc\,\%). The MOF-like design trains
on half as many materials, but the smooth design's learning curve predicts only a
\(\nLCHalfFactor\times\) larger error for that, against the \(\nVthreeOverVtwo\times\)
observed: the harder isotherms, not the smaller training set, account for most of the drop.

A ranking that needs no solve does much worse in both designs. Ordering the materials by their stoichiometric time,
which needs only the isotherm, gives a Spearman correlation of \nBaseVtwoSp\ (smooth) and
\nBaseVthreeSp\ (MOF-like), with recalls of \nBaseVtwoRc\ and \nBaseVthreeRc\,\%. Every arm
is handed the run horizon, which was extended for \nExtVtwoPct\,\% of the smooth design's
runs because breakthrough was incomplete; ranking by that horizon alone gives a Spearman
correlation of \nLeakVtwoSp, so it carries almost none of the ranking. The intervals
resample materials with replacement, which duplicates some and biases rank statistics
slightly downward, so the adequacy verdicts are conservative.

\subsection{Six published frameworks}
\label{sec:frameworks}

The held-out materials above are random points of a sampled box. To test real adsorbents,
six water-harvesting frameworks were parameterised from their primary literature by a rule fixed before the runs:
capacity, heat of adsorption and step humidity as the means of the stated values, and the
step steepness from the stated rise interval, \(n = \ln 81/\ln(p_{90}/p_{10})\), or the
design median where no interval is stated. The formulation parameters take the design
medians; on the MOF-like design \(f_H\) is the below-step fraction the sources report, and
the affinity ratio is the design median. Supplementary S2 lists every input with its
source, and Supplementary S1 the places where the rule's application departs from its
registered wording (entry B88), none of which moves a framework across a design boundary.
Each framework was solved at the \nDataNconditions\ dataset conditions, skipping the cells
the dataset's own filter rejects, and both surrogates were refit once on all materials of
a design. A framework is \emph{inside the held-out error distribution} when its mean error
is at most the 95th percentile of the same arm's per-material errors in the five-fold test
(on the smooth design, \nFwDonPninetyfive\,\% for DeepONet and \nFwHgbPninetyfive\,\% for POD
with boosting).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Where the uncertainty lies} Every surrogate and coarse-solve error in this work
is measured against the reference solver, not against experiment. Against a measured
MOF-303 bed that solver reproduces the half-breakthrough time but not the early leak
(Section~\ref{sec:model}), so for a real screen the model of the adsorbent is a larger
source of error than the choice between a surrogate and a coarse solve. Better isotherm and
kinetic descriptions, including loading-dependent transport and micropore-controlled
kinetics \cite{hastings2024high,sircar2000why}, would do more for a real screen than a
better surrogate of the present model, and screening at the process level rather than on
material indicators \cite{farmahini2021performance} requires them.

\paragraph{What to improve} Where surrogates are worth building, the measurements point to
the coefficient map and to the training design, not to richer bases, Kolmogorov--Arnold
layers or heavy physics losses. Joint training of basis and coefficients, as in DeepONet,
is worth a constant factor over per-mode regression, and how the parameters enter a model
governs how far it extrapolates \cite{vantegelen2026late}; structure-aware inputs to the
map, for example isotherms predicted from framework structure \cite{goeminne2025ab}, are a
natural next lever. The training design matters at least as much as the model: the same
architecture that fails on CAU-10-H when trained on smooth isotherms predicts it within its
held-out error when the design contains steep steps.

\paragraph{Limitations} The held-out materials are drawn from the same design space as the
training materials, so the main results are interpolation statements; the named-framework
test probes extrapolation for six materials only. The model describes one adsorption step
from a clean bed, near equilibrium, in one dimension, with no desorption, hysteresis,
cycling or heat-transfer-limited device geometry \cite{lapotin2019adsorption,delange2015adsorption};
the half-breakthrough time of one step is a column quantity, not a harvester's figure of
merit such as working capacity or water yield per day. The heat of adsorption does not
depend on loading, the bed heat capacity omits the adsorbed water, and the step is placed
at each material's step humidity at the feed temperature, which removes the temperature
dependence of the step position that matters for harvesting \cite{hastings2024high}. The
framework parameters are read from the literature by a rule, not fitted to measured
isotherms, and the MOF-like design uses one training seed except for DeepONet at
\(p = 16\). Benchmark conclusions for MOF models can change with sample size
\cite{abaei2026benchmark}, which is why the 12-material comparisons are labelled
exploratory. The pre-registrations are attested by the authors' repository history and its
public push events, not by an external registry.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

The basis error and the map error of a surrogate on held-out materials can be measured
separately, and for a MOF water-adsorption bed they are far apart: the coefficient map is
the limit, on smooth and on MOF-like isotherms alike, and a richer basis buys nothing. For
a one-dimensional column the practical consequence is that a coarse numerical solve is the
better screening tool. Surrogates earn their place only where a solve is expensive, and
there their accuracy on real frameworks depends on whether the training design contains
the strong sites and steep steps those frameworks have.

Three directions follow. Coefficient maps that receive isotherms predicted from structure,
rather than fitted parameters, could carry information the parameters lack. Device models
with desorption, cycling and heat transfer are where the cost balance could turn, and where
the same diagnostic should be repeated. And the model of the adsorbent itself, with
loading-dependent heat and transport and a step that moves with temperature, limits a real
screen more than any surrogate does. The protocol, data and code are public, so each of
these can be tested in the same way.
